I had a conversation recently with a friend and colleague whom I deeply respect. Someone who's read my blog posts and told me they enjoyed my writing. We were talking about my process, and I mentioned that I use an LLM as part of it.

Their response surprised me: "This really affects how I see your writing."

The work hadn't changed. My ideas hadn't changed. The words that resonated with them before were the same words. But now, knowing an LLM was involved in my process, it felt different to them.

"It just feels like I liked the robot," they said.

That reaction made me start thinking: What are we actually responding to when we have feelings about AI-assisted work? And are those responses consistent across different contexts?


## We've been here before

This isn't the first time a new writing tool has been met with resistance. The typewriter was initially seen as impersonal and mechanical. "Real" writers used pen and paper. Word processors were accused of making writers lazy. Spell check was going to ruin our ability to spell. Grammar checkers would make us dependent on machines for basic language skills.

Each time, people argued that the new tool would somehow diminish the authenticity or value of the writing. Each time, we eventually accepted that the tool was just that: a tool. The thinking, the ideas, the authorship remained with the human.

So what makes AI different? Or does it just feel different because it's new and we haven't made our peace with it yet?


## The double standard

In the tech world, using AI to help write code has become mostly accepted. Developers use GitHub Copilot or ChatGPT to generate code, review the output, modify it, and ship it. Generally, no one questions whether they "really" wrote that code.

The developer is still making the architectural decisions. They're still reviewing every line. They're still responsible for whether it works. They're still the one who understands what problem they're solving and why. The AI is just helping with syntax and boilerplate.

But using an LLM to help organize your writing? Different reaction entirely.

In both cases, the human is providing the thinking, the judgment, and the final decision making. The AI is assisting with the mechanical part. So why does one feel acceptable and the other doesn't?

Maybe it's about context. In professional settings, clients and employers care about deliverables. If a proposal is well-written, does the client care how you organized it? If code works and solves the problem, does it matter how the syntax was generated? We tend to judge professional work by its results.

But writing, especially personal or creative writing, feels different. We attach moral weight to the process itself.


## What are we actually valuing?

This raises a fundamental question: Do the results matter, or does how we achieve them matter more?

If a piece of code works perfectly, solves the problem elegantly, and is well-architected, is it less valuable because a developer used AI assistance to write it? Most people would say no. Working code is working code.

But if a piece of writing clearly communicates ideas, resonates with readers, and accurately represents the author's thinking, is it less valuable because the author used AI to help organize it? Here, people are less sure. There seems to be a stronger sense that the process itself has moral weight.

Why the difference? Maybe because we see writing as more personal, more human, more connected to identity. Using a tool to help with code feels practical. Using a tool to help with writing feels like cheating.

Or maybe it depends on what kind of writing we're talking about. Should we have different standards for poetry versus technical documentation? For fiction versus blog posts? For creative work versus utilitarian communication?

A novel feels deeply personal. The prose itself is part of the art. But a technical blog post or a piece of documentation? The goal there is clarity and communication. If AI helps achieve that goal, does the process matter as much as the result?


## What "writing" actually is

Let's break down what goes into creating a piece of writing:

- Generating the ideas and thesis
- Developing supporting arguments
- Organizing chaos into structure
- Ensuring voice and accuracy
- Making editorial judgment calls

If someone does four out of five of those things entirely themselves, and uses an LLM to help with organizing structure, did they write it? Or was it the AI?

Did Hemingway not write his novels because Maxwell Perkins edited them? We accept that creation involves tools and collaboration. So where's the line?


## Intent matters

Here's a distinction I think is important: There's a meaningful difference between "AI, write me a blog post about this topic" and "AI, here are my thoughts, help me organize them."

One is asking AI to think for you. The other is asking it to help you communicate your thinking.

One is generation. The other is assistance with execution.

Does that distinction matter? I think it does. The person who brings their own thesis, arguments, and ideas to the AI and then edits the output heavily is doing fundamentally different work than the person who asks the AI to generate ideas for them.

But I'm not sure everyone sees it that way. To some people, any AI involvement at all feels like the line has been crossed.


## AI as a tool

An LLM is a tool. I wouldn't trust an LLM to come up with my ideas any more than I would trust a hammer to design a house, or a screwdriver to decide what to build. I wouldn't trust it to tell me what to think or how to approach a problem.

Those are human responsibilities. My responsibilities.

What I do trust it for is the thing it's actually good at: helping me take ideas I already have and organize them in a way that works for linear, neurotypical communication. That's it. That's the job.

When people worry about AI "replacing" human thinking or creativity, I think they're imagining a very different use case than what I'm describing. They're imagining someone outsourcing their thinking to a machine. And yeah, that would be a problem.

But using a tool to help execute your thinking more effectively? That's just using tools the way humans always have.


## The accessibility angle

Here's where it gets more complicated for me personally. I'm neurodivergent. My brain doesn't work in straight lines. My thinking is abstract and systemic. I see the whole interconnected web at once, not a sequence of logical steps.

I've always struggled to translate what's in my head into linear prose that other people can parse. It's not that I can't think clearly, it's that the way I think clearly doesn't match the way neurotypical communication works.

So I use an LLM to help bridge that gap. I come to it with my thesis and arguments already formed. It helps me organize the chaos into something that follows neurotypical writing conventions. Then I edit heavily to make sure it says exactly what I meant.

This raises questions I don't think we talk about enough: Are ideas less valuable if someone needs a tool to express them in neurotypical formats? 

We don't question whether someone using a screen reader is "really reading." We don't say that speech-to-text makes someone's words less authentic. We accept Grammarly as an assistive tool. But an LLM that helps with organization? That feels different to people.

Why? Where exactly is the line between acceptable assistive technology and unacceptable automation?


## The transparency question

Should creators disclose when they've used AI? This question comes up a lot, and I don't think there's a clear answer.

If I use an LLM to help organize my thoughts, should I disclose that? If so, should I also disclose when I use Grammarly? Spell check? When I've worked with an editor? When I've talked through ideas with a friend who helped me clarify my thinking?

What level of tool assistance requires disclosure?

The assumption behind demanding disclosure seems to be that AI assistance makes work less valuable or less authentic. But if you can't tell the difference without being told, what exactly is being devalued?

I'm not arguing against transparency. I'm asking: what are we actually trying to achieve with it? Are we trying to help readers make informed judgments about quality? Or are we just uncomfortable with the idea of AI involvement and want to know so we can dismiss the work?


## The MacAdmin community

I've noticed something specific to the MacAdmin community: there's a particularly strong knee-jerk reaction that anything AI automatically equals bad. I see it more in MacAdmin circles than in other tech spaces I'm part of.

Maybe it's about craft. Our community values deep technical knowledge and hands-on expertise. There's pride in understanding systems at a fundamental level. AI tools can feel like they're short-circuiting that understanding.

Maybe it's about control. We work in a field where knowing exactly what's happening under the hood matters. AI is opaque in ways that make many of us uncomfortable.

Maybe it's about fear. Not necessarily fear of being replaced, but fear of what it means for human identity and value if AI can help with things we've always considered fundamentally human.

Maybe it's just new and we don't trust it yet. We've seen enough tech hype cycles to be skeptical.

But here's what's interesting to me: people who have that strong reaction will still tell me they love my blog posts. The content resonates. The ideas are useful. The voice feels authentic.

Until they find out an LLM touched it somewhere in the process. Then suddenly it feels less real, even though nothing about the actual content changed, just the knowledge of how it was made.


## The experiment

I'm not going to tell you which parts of this post involved an LLM. I'm not going to explain my exact process for this specific piece.

Does that bother you? Does it make you want to analyze every sentence, looking for tells? Does it make you trust this post less?

If you can't tell the difference, what exactly are you reacting to? The work itself? Or just the idea that AI might have been involved?

The ideas in this post are mine. The arguments are mine. This post represents what I actually think. I stand by every word of it.

So did I write this? Or did an LLM?

Maybe the better question is: does it matter if you can't tell?
